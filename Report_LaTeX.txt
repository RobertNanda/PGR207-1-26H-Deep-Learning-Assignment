\begin{abstract}
Deep learning models for computer vision are highly sensitive to architectural configurations, data augmentation regimens, and gradient-based optimization algorithms. This comprehensive report presents a systematic, one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) empirical investigation on the CIFAR-10 image classification benchmark to determine the independent and relative effects of these three fundamental axes. A custom Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) serves as the experimental baseline, which is rigorously compared against two modern, highly parameterized architectures: MobileNetV3-Large and DenseNet121. Concurrently, the study evaluates the impact of training data transformations by contrasting a basic photometric baseline with two advanced strategies, including a pipeline featuring vertical flipping and a robust algorithmic approach utilizing RandAugment. Finally, the research analyzes optimization dynamics by comparing Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with momentum against adaptive learning rate methods, specifically Adam and AdamW. Through strict control of hyperparameters across twenty-one distinct training runs—comprising seven configurations initialized across three random seeds—the analysis isolates the causal variables driving classification accuracy. The empirical evidence demonstrates that model architecture constitutes the most dominant factor governing absolute performance, with DenseNet121 achieving a superior top-1 accuracy of 84.44\% $\pm$ 0.48\%, significantly surpassing the baseline's 75.18\% $\pm$ 0.20\%. However, this performance is achieved at a substantial computational penalty. The data augmentation study reveals that out-of-distribution transformations, such as vertical flipping, catastrophically degraded performance to 69.80\% $\pm$ 0.29\%. The optimizer evaluation establishes that adaptive methods yield significantly faster convergence over SGD within a constrained 25-epoch budget. The findings emphasize the critical interdependency between a network's representational capacity and the requisite regularization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The classification of low-resolution natural images remains a critical benchmark for evaluating the representational capabilities of deep neural networks. The CIFAR-10 dataset, consisting of 60,000 $32 \times 32$ pixel color images distributed equally across ten mutually exclusive categorical classes, presents a uniquely constrained environment \cite{cifar10}. The low spatial resolution coupled with high intra-class variance forces classification algorithms to rely on robust hierarchical feature extraction rather than superficial pixel-level matching. Advancing state-of-the-art performance on such benchmarks requires a precise calibration of the network's architectural capacity, the regularization mechanisms applied to the training distribution, and the efficiency of the numerical optimization landscape.

This report documents an exhaustive investigation into the isolated effects of three distinct deep learning factors—model architecture, data augmentation, and optimizer choice—on CIFAR-10 classification efficacy. Modern deep learning research frequently confounds these variables, introducing novel architectures alongside highly optimized, heavily augmented training pipelines, rendering it impossible to attribute performance gains to specific innovations. To rectify this, our experimental framework is structured around a rigorous One-Factor-At-A-Time (OFAT) design. 

Our investigation aims to answer the following core research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Architecture):} To what extent do deep, structurally complex architectures (DenseNet121, MobileNetV3) improve classification accuracy over a shallow baseline CNN when trained from random initialization, and how does this scale with computational cost?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Augmentation):} Do advanced algorithmic data augmentation techniques (RandAugment) provide superior regularization and narrower generalization gaps compared to standard geometric transformations, and what is the effect of semantic-destroying transforms like vertical flipping?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Optimization):} Do adaptive moment estimation methods (Adam, AdamW) result in accelerated convergence velocities and higher final accuracies than traditional Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with momentum under a tightly restricted training epoch budget?
\end{enumerate}

We hypothesize that (H1) highly parameterized architectures will outperform the baseline but will require proportionally greater training time; (H2) RandAugment will suppress training accuracy but elevate validation accuracy by forcing the network to learn invariant features, whereas vertical flipping will destroy performance by violating natural image gravity priors; and (H3) adaptive optimizers will reach early stopping criteria significantly faster than SGD due to parameter-specific learning rate scaling.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Architectural Paradigms and Network Capacity}
The architectural evolution of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) has been fundamentally driven by the mathematical imperative to extract complex hierarchical features while mitigating the vanishing gradient problem inherent to extremely deep networks. The introduction of Residual Networks (ResNets) revolutionized the field by implementing identity skip connections \cite{resnet}. Mathematically, instead of hoping each few stacked layers directly fit a desired underlying mapping $\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})$, ResNets explicitly let these layers fit a residual mapping $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) := \mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{x}$. The original mapping is recast as $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}$, which allows gradients to flow unimpeded backward through the addition operator, enabling networks of unprecedented depth.

Building upon the residual learning paradigm, Densely Connected Convolutional Networks (DenseNets) proposed a radical connectivity pattern \cite{densenet}. While traditional convolutional networks with $L$ layers possess $L$ connections, DenseNets contain $\frac{L(L+1)}{2}$ connections. Let $\mathbf{x}_l$ be the output of the $l^{th}$ layer. DenseNets concatenate the feature maps of all preceding layers as input:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_l = H_l([\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{l-1}])
\end{equation}
where $H_l(\cdot)$ represents a composite function of Batch Normalization, ReLU, and Convolution. This dense connectivity structure effectively alleviates the vanishing gradient problem, dramatically strengthens feature propagation, encourages the reuse of low-level features, and substantially reduces the total number of trainable parameters compared to equivalent ResNet architectures.

In contrast to the parameter-heavy pursuit of maximal accuracy, the deployment of deep learning models on edge devices catalyzed the development of lightweight architectures. MobileNetV3 represents the culmination of hardware-aware Neural Architecture Search (NAS) \cite{mobilenetv3}. The architecture integrates inverted residual blocks with linear bottlenecks, leveraging depthwise separable convolutions to drastically reduce computational complexity. Furthermore, MobileNetV3 incorporates Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) modules directly within the residual layers to adaptively recalibrate channel-wise feature responses, and utilizes the hard-swish activation function for optimized hardware latency. Evaluating DenseNet121 against MobileNetV3 and a traditional CNN baseline provides a comprehensive spectrum of how architectural density interacts with the CIFAR-10 data manifold.

\subsection{The Mechanics of Data Augmentation}
Data augmentation serves as a critical, explicit regularization technique designed to artificially inflate the volumetric diversity of the training dataset. By applying stochastic transformations to the input data, augmentation inhibits the network's ability to memorize the training distribution (overfitting), thereby enforcing the learning of robust, invariant features that generalize to unseen test data \cite{aug_survey}. Traditional augmentation pipelines rely heavily on simple geometric transformations—such as random cropping and horizontal flipping—and photometric adjustments, including color jittering and brightness shifting.

Recently, the field has pivoted toward automated, policy-based augmentation strategies. RandAugment drastically reduces the search space of optimal policies to merely two interpretable, scalar hyperparameters: the number of transformation operations applied sequentially to an image ($N$), and a global magnitude controlling the distortion severity for all operations ($M$) \cite{randaugment}. By uniformly sampling from a standard list of transformations (e.g., identity, rotate, solarize, color, posterize, contrast, brightness, sharpness, shear, translate) and applying them at magnitude $M$, RandAugment scales effectively with model size without requiring a separate proxy search phase.

Additionally, spatial masking techniques such as Cutout have proven highly effective \cite{cutout}. Cutout regularizes the network by randomly masking a square region of the input image with zero or mean pixel values during training. This forces the convolutional layers to rely on a broader, more distributed set of contextual features rather than converging entirely on a single highly discriminative visual cue. Investigating how deep networks respond to advanced algorithmic pipelines (RandAugment) versus highly destructive, out-of-distribution geometric manipulations (such as vertical flipping, which violently violates the natural gravity prior of terrestrial images) sheds light on the strict boundaries of effective regularization.

\subsection{Optimization Landscapes and Gradient Descent}
The numerical optimization of stochastic objective functions in deep neural networks has traditionally been dominated by Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) utilizing Nesterov momentum \cite{momentum}. Momentum accelerates the gradient vectors in directions of consistent descent while dampening high-frequency oscillations across steep loss ravines, providing excellent long-term generalization.

Adaptive learning rate algorithms were developed to resolve SGD's sensitivity to global learning rates. Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation) computes individual learning rates for every parameter based on exponential moving averages of both the first moment $\mathbf{m}_t$ (the mean) and the second moment $\mathbf{v}_t$ (the uncentered variance) of the gradients \cite{adam}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{m}_t = \beta_1 \mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1) \mathbf{g}_t
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v}_t = \beta_2 \mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2) \mathbf{g}_t^2
\end{equation}
While Adam demonstrates rapid initial convergence, empirical evidence suggests it often yields inferior generalization on image classification tasks compared to carefully tuned SGD. This discrepancy was traced to the mathematical implementation of $L_2$ regularization. In standard SGD, $L_2$ regularization and weight decay are mathematically equivalent; however, in adaptive algorithms like Adam, the gradient modification caused by $L_2$ regularization is scaled by the inverse of the second moment, diminishing the decay effect on parameters with large gradients. AdamW resolves this by decoupling the weight decay from the gradient update, applying the decay directly to the weights at each step \cite{adamw}. This symmetrical regularization restores the generalization benefits traditionally monopolized by SGD while preserving the rapid convergence velocity of Adam.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Dataset Configuration and Deterministic Preprocessing}
The CIFAR-10 dataset comprises 60,000 images, partitioned into a training set of 50,000 images and a test set of 10,000 images \cite{cifar10}. To facilitate rigorous hyperparameter selection, optimization monitoring, and early stopping without risking data leakage, the 50,000 training images were subjected to a deterministic, class-stratified split utilizing a predefined random state (seed 42). Exactly 90\% (45,000 images) were allocated to the active training subset, while 10\% (5,000 images) were strictly held out to form the validation subset. The 10,000-image test set remained completely sequestered throughout the training phase.

A standard, deterministic preprocessing transformation was uniformly applied to all validation and test images across every experimental run. The images were instantiated as 32-bit floating-point tensors and subjected to pixel-wise normalization using a mean vector of $(0.5, 0.5, 0.5)$ and a standard deviation vector of $(0.5, 0.5, 0.5)$ across the RGB color channels, scaling the pixel distributions into the $[-1.0, 1.0]$ range to stabilize the initial gradient flow. We explicitly avoided resizing the images or utilizing pre-trained ImageNet weights, as doing so would compress the performance distribution and obscure the specific effects of the factors under study.

\subsection{One-Factor-At-A-Time (OFAT) Experimental Design}
To ensure that performance deltas could be causally attributed to specific algorithmic changes, the study was rigidly structured around a One-Factor-At-A-Time (OFAT) matrix. A singular, shared "Baseline" configuration (C1) was established. In each subsequent factor study, exactly one component—architecture, augmentation, or optimizer—was modified while all other hyperparameters, hardware environments, data splits, and seeds remained completely identical. 

The matrix generated seven distinct configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C1 (Baseline):} Plain CNN, Baseline Augmentation, SGD + Momentum.
    \item \textbf{C2:} MobileNetV3, Baseline Augmentation, SGD + Momentum.
    \item \textbf{C3:} DenseNet121, Baseline Augmentation, SGD + Momentum.
    \item \textbf{C4:} Plain CNN, Aug1 (Crop + Flip + Rot), SGD + Momentum.
    \item \textbf{C5:} Plain CNN, Aug2 (RandAugment), SGD + Momentum.
    \item \textbf{C6:} Plain CNN, Baseline Augmentation, Adam.
    \item \textbf{C7:} Plain CNN, Baseline Augmentation, AdamW.
\end{itemize}

To rigorously account for stochastic variance, each of the seven configurations was independently executed using three explicit random seeds (0, 1, 2). This generated a total of 21 unique training runs. The seed controlled the pseudo-random number generators for weight initialization, data loader shuffling, and stochastic augmentation operations.

\subsection{Factor 1: Architectural Variants}
1. \textbf{Baseline Simple CNN (C1, C4-C7):} A sequential architecture featuring four consecutive convolutional layers (channel depths 32, 32, 64, 140) utilizing LeakyReLU and ReLU activations. Feature maps are downsampled via two $2 \times 2$ Max Pooling layers and regularized with 2D Dropout ($p=0.2$). The classification head flattens the tensor into an 8,960-dimensional vector, funneling through a 512-dimensional hidden layer with dropout ($p=0.5$), culminating in a 10-class linear output. Trainable parameters: 4,702,582.

2. \textbf{MobileNetV3-Large (C2):} An advanced architecture built upon inverted residual blocks. Because MobileNetV3 was originally optimized for $224 \times 224$ ImageNet inputs, applying it directly to $32 \times 32$ CIFAR-10 images causes premature destruction of spatial resolution. To rectify this, the initial standard convolution was surgically altered to utilize a $3 \times 3$ kernel with a stride of 1, preventing the immediate downsampling of the input tensor. Trainable parameters: 4,214,842.

3. \textbf{DenseNet121 (C3):} A deeply interconnected architecture. Similar to the MobileNet adaptation, the macroscopic stem of the DenseNet was modified for the $32 \times 32$ domain. The initial $7 \times 7$ convolutional layer was replaced with a $3 \times 3$ convolution (stride 1, padding 1), and the subsequent max-pooling operation was replaced with an Identity layer, preserving the critical $32 \times 32$ feature map for the first dense block. Trainable parameters: 6,956,426.

\subsection{Factor 2: Data Augmentation Regimens}
Augmentation policies were applied exclusively to the training dataset. 
1. \textbf{Baseline Augmentation (C1-C3, C6-C7):} A strictly photometric transformation sequence. Input images were subjected to random photometric distortions, a random grayscale conversion ($p=0.2$), and a random pixel inversion ($p=0.2$). Spatial geometries remained untouched.
2. \textbf{Aug1 (C4):} A highly aggressive, geometrically disruptive policy. The sequence applied a random crop to $32 \times 32$ pixels following a 4-pixel padding, random rotations of up to 20 degrees, complex color jittering (brightness 0.2, contrast 0.2, saturation 0.2, hue 0.1), and crucially, random \textit{vertical flipping}.
3. \textbf{Aug2 (C5):} An automated regularization pipeline. The sequence utilized random cropping with a generous 6-pixel padding, standard random horizontal flipping, and the implementation of RandAugment configured with $N=1$ operations and a severe magnitude of $M=18$.

\subsection{Factor 3: Optimization Algorithms}
All configurations minimized a categorical Cross-Entropy Loss function over a maximum budget of 25 epochs.
1. \textbf{Baseline SGD (C1-C5):} Stochastic Gradient Descent utilizing a learning rate of $\eta = 0.01$ and Nesterov momentum of $0.9$.
2. \textbf{Adam (C6):} Initialized with a learning rate of $\eta = 0.001$. This specific learning rate was identified via a preliminary validation search, as Adam requires significantly smaller step sizes than SGD.
3. \textbf{AdamW (C7):} Adam utilizing decoupled weight decay. The learning rate remained $\eta = 0.001$, with a strict weight decay penalty of $\lambda = 0.01$.

The batch size was universally fixed at 128. A dynamic learning rate scheduler (\texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau}) monitored the validation loss with a patience of 3 epochs, multiplying the learning rate by 0.1 upon stagnation. An early stopping mechanism was engaged with a patience of 6 epochs. All runs were executed using PyTorch 2.0+ \cite{pytorch} on a CUDA-accelerated backend.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
The primary performance metrics are Top-1 Accuracy and Macro-averaged F1 Score. The Macro-F1 score computes the harmonic mean of precision and recall independently for each of the ten classes, then calculates the unweighted arithmetic mean, ensuring the metric is not artificially inflated by high performance on easily separable classes:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Macro-F1} = \frac{1}{10} \sum_{c=1}^{10} 2 \cdot \frac{P_c \cdot R_c}{P_c + R_c}
\end{equation}
We also compute class-wise precision and recall, alongside detailed confusion matrices for the best and worst performing configurations. All final aggregated metrics are reported as the arithmetic mean $\pm$ the sample standard deviation (with $n-1$ degrees of freedom) computed across the three random seeds.

The highly sophisticated NAS-derived architecture simply possessed too much capacity for the naive photometric baseline augmentation, allowing it to instantly memorize the training distribution rather than learning invariant spatial features.

The application of spatial augmentation drastically rewrote the training dynamics. Aug1 triggered a catastrophic collapse in performance, plummeting the test accuracy to 69.80\% (a 5.38\% deficit compared to the baseline). CIFAR-10 contains classes bound by strict real-world gravity priors (e.g., cars sit on roads, airplanes fly in the sky). Vertical flipping forces the network to learn nonsensical orientations, irreparably corrupting the spatial feature hierarchy.

Aug2, leveraging the RandAugment algorithm ($M=18$), generated a marginal accuracy improvement to 75.63\%. However, the critical finding lies in the generalization gap. The severe magnitude of the RandAugment distortions artificially suppressed the training accuracy. Yet, because the network was forced to learn robust features through the distortion noise, these features mapped incredibly well to the clean validation set, indicating extreme, highly effective regularization.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

The systematic execution of this empirical study yields nuanced answers to our primary research questions governing CIFAR-10 classification dynamics.

\textbf{RQ1 (Architecture):} The empirical evidence decisively supports the hypothesis that model architecture exerts the largest absolute effect on classification performance. The $9.26\%$ performance delta established by DenseNet121 over the baseline CNN is nearly twenty times larger than the observed seed-to-seed variance ($\pm 0.20\%$ to $\pm 0.48\%$), confirming the mathematical superiority of dense feature map concatenation. However, deploying high-capacity architectures without scaling the intensity of the data augmentation leads to rapid overfitting, as observed with MobileNetV3's 76.40\% accuracy despite its vast theoretical capacity.

\textbf{RQ2 (Augmentation):} The data augmentation hypothesis is supported regarding semantic preservation. The implementation of RandAugment (Aug2) provided massive regularization, but the baseline CNN lacked the structural capacity to decode the extreme variance injected by a magnitude 18 policy, resulting in only a $+0.45\%$ gain. Conversely, the destructive failure of the Aug1 pipeline ($-5.38\%$ accuracy) unequivocally demonstrates that transformations must preserve the semantic and physical priors of the underlying data; vertical flipping destroys the spatial reasoning required for natural image classification.

\textbf{RQ3 (Optimization):} Under the strict 25-epoch budget, both Adam and AdamW significantly outperformed SGD in absolute accuracy ($+1.25\%$) and convergence velocity (terminating 4-5 epochs earlier). Adaptive learning rates successfully navigated the gradient topology faster than traditional momentum. The theoretical superiority of AdamW (decoupled weight decay) is generally realized over highly extended training schedules (100+ epochs); here, early stopping triggered before AdamW could mathematically separate from standard Adam.

\textbf{Limitations:} The primary limitation of this research is the rigidity of the 25-epoch training budget. Advanced regularization algorithms like RandAugment typically require highly extended training regimes (200+ epochs) to allow the network to converge over the violently shifting data manifold. Furthermore, the OFAT methodology inherently prevents the analysis of cross-variable interactions (e.g., MobileNetV3 + RandAugment might solve its overfitting). Lastly, fixing the learning rate uniformly across different optimizers inherently biases the comparison, as SGD and Adam operate optimally in vastly different hyperparameter spaces.

In conclusion, scaling deep learning performance on low-resolution benchmarks is a multivariate challenge. While architectural density dictates the theoretical ceiling of a model, it is the data augmentation strategy that governs whether that capacity is utilized for generalized understanding or rote memorization, and it is the optimizer that dictates the computational cost of reaching convergence.
